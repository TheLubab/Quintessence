## The big idea: two worlds

Split the system into two parts.

- **Shell:** fixed, written by you, never touched by the AI.
- **Cards:** generated by the AI, living inside the shell.

This doesn't destroy the point of the product. It protects it. The early Mac menu bar was always there, so you could never get lost. Your shell does the same job. If the AI is slow, wrong, or offline, the user can still go home, undo, close a card, or stop a generation.

Keep the shell very small. Only put in things that must work when the AI fails:

- Home (the intent screen)
- A free text box (so people who know what they want can just type)
- Undo and history
- A list of open cards, so you can switch between them
- Stop, pin, close, delete

Everything else is AI territory. Each fixed thing is something the AI can't adapt, so be strict.

## Names

Here are names I'd try. Rename anything you don't like.

| Part      | Meaning                                          | Inspired by          |
| --------- | ------------------------------------------------ | -------------------- |
| **Desk**  | The whole user world: all cards, layers, data    | Mac desktop          |
| **Shell** | The fixed frame, with no AI                      | Menu bar             |
| **Card**  | One self-contained mini-app                      | HyperCard            |
| **Layer** | A depth level that cards sit on                  | Your background idea |
| **Thing** | A piece of real data (a note, a CSV, an article) | Files                |
| **Link**  | A connection between cards or things             | Linux pipes          |

The picture looks like this:

```
Desk
├─ Shell (fixed)
├─ Layers
│   ├─ Ground   (always there, calm, ambient)
│   ├─ Main     (the cards you work in)
│   └─ Overlay  (temporary: popups, sheets)
├─ Cards (unlimited)
└─ Things (the user's real data)
```

I'd keep the layer types few and fixed, three or so. The user and the AI add many cards, not many layers. If layers can multiply, the AI starts inventing structure and the Desk gets messy.

## Why it never slows down

Slowdown comes from one thing: the AI having to read everything. Cards fix this because each card is its own small world.

1. **Each card has its own state and its own history.** When you act inside a card, the AI sees that card in full. It sees all the other cards only as one-line summaries, like a table of contents. The cost depends on the size of one card, never on the size of the Desk.
2. **Cards can sleep.** Cards you haven't touched are saved to disk. They aren't drawn and they aren't in the AI's context. They wake up when needed. You could have ten thousand cards and only five are alive.
3. **Every card keeps a gist.** It's one short line that says what the card is and what's in it. The AI reads gists to understand the Desk.
4. **History gets folded.** Every so often the long list of changes becomes one snapshot, so loading stays fast.
5. **Big cards split.** When a card gets too large, it becomes a parent with child cards. Size has a natural limit.
6. **Big content is stored by reference.** A 50-page article lives as a Thing, and the card holds only a handle to it.

## Most clicks should never reach the AI

This is the biggest speed trick, and HyperCard already had it. In HyperCard, a click travels up a chain: button, then card, then background, then stack, then Home. The first one that knows what to do handles it.

You can do the same:

1. The card handles small things itself: switching tabs, expanding, scrolling, selecting. These are instant and cost nothing.
2. The shell handles universal things: back, undo, close.
3. Only a real new intent falls through to the AI.

Cards would carry small built-in reactions, so the AI says "this is a tab group" and the engine knows how tabs behave. The AI decides what things are, and the engine decides how they respond. That gives you instant feel, saves tokens, and gives muscle memory, because a tab always behaves like a tab.

## Self-contained but connected

Cards must not reach inside each other. If they did, one broken card would break others. They connect in three ways, all borrowed from old systems:

1. **Shared Things.** The data lives outside the cards. A CSV is a Thing, and one card shows it as a table while another shows it as a chart. Edit it in one and the other updates. This is the main connection.
2. **Drag and drop, and clipboard.** The user moves a Thing between cards, like on the early Mac.
3. **A few standard messages.** A card can say "something was selected" or "open this", and other cards can listen. Keep the list short and fixed, like a small set of system events.

## The design grammar is your Human Interface Guidelines

Apple's first big win was that every Mac app looked and behaved the same way, because the rules were written down. Your design grammar plays that role. The AI says "this is important, this is a list, this is a primary action", and the engine makes it look good and behave consistently across all cards. That matches your belief that the AI shouldn't design.

## One thing to decide early

Your plan says the AI runs continuously. I'd run it per event and per card. Wake it when a real intent arrives, give it one card plus the table of contents, let it answer, and let it sleep. A continuously running AI watching a growing Desk is the one design that would eventually slow down.

The next question is who decides where cards sit on the screen: the AI, the user, or a fixed rule based on attention level. That choice shapes how the Main layer works. Which direction feels right to you?
